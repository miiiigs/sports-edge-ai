Sports Edge
A fully autonomous, multi-agent AI betting desk — technical overview for developers
Internal architecture briefing · prepared for a technical / engineering reader · July 2026
Sports Edge is a sports-betting information business that is operated almost end-to-end by a team of
specialised AI agents. What makes it interesting from an engineering standpoint is not the betting —
it's that the entire back office (research, publishing, settlement, auditing, supervision, customer ops)
is implemented as a set of scheduled Claude agents that coordinate through a shared
document store, with strict separation of duties, no central application server, and a human kept
deliberately in the loop for the one irreversible action: placing money.
1 · What the product actually is
The product is not "winning picks." It is a verifiable, honest track record. The whole thesis is that the
tipster market is full of unverifiable records, deleted losses, and cherry-picked screenshots — so Sports
Edge competes on trust: every selection is published before the event starts with a stated minimum
acceptable price, every result (including losses and voids) is recorded and never edited, and the desk
grades itself publicly on closing-line value (whether the market later agreed its price was sharp) rather
than on win rate. "No bet today" is treated as a valid, publishable product.
Current maturity (stated honestly): a handful of days of live record, ~18 settled bets, small positive P/L. The
desk's own auditor calls this variance, not proven edge. Everything in the design — the gates, the caps, the
never-delete rules — follows from taking that uncertainty seriously.
2 · The core architectural idea
There is no traditional backend. Each "employee" is an independent AI agent that wakes on a schedule
(a cron-style trigger), does a narrow job, writes its results into shared files, and exits. The design borrows
from how a real, well-governed company separates responsibilities:
Separation of duties. No single agent selects a bet, publishes it, settles it, and reports on it. The
analyst cannot settle its own bets; the settlement agent cannot pick or re-price; the auditor cannot be
edited by the desk it audits.
Shared state instead of an API. Coordination happens through a versioned knowledge base of
markdown/CSV/HTML documents (the "project"). Every writer stamps a timestamp and its sources; the
last writer reconciles running totals. This is effectively an append-and-reconcile ledger, not a mutable
database.
Management by exception. Supervisory agents stay silent when everything is correct and only emit a
notification when a rule is violated — so the human sees signal, not noise.
Human-in-the-loop for irreversible actions. The agents research, price, publish and settle, but a
person places every wager by hand and reports back the actual odds taken. No agent ever moves
money.

4 · Data model / shared state
The "system of record" is a set of files, not a database. The important ones:
ledger.md — the human-readable source of truth: every card, every settled result with two sources,
running units/dollars/bankroll, an ops log of changed-runs, and a source-reliability flag archive.
bets.csv — a structured mirror of every bet (id, odds, stake, est/no-vig probability, close, outcome,
P/L, status) for programmatic reconciliation and calibration.
live-dashboard-template.html — a self-contained dashboard that fetches live scores from a public
feed in the reader's browser; the agents re-send it after material changes.
Rule/knowledge docs — staking rules, pro-betting principles, the org charter, source playbooks,
calibration code. These act as durable long-term memory that every future agent run reads before
acting.
Reconciliation is disciplined rather than transactional: writers stamp time and source, changed-only
logging avoids churn, and the auditor independently re-derives totals weekly. Divergence between
ledger.md and bets.csv is a finding, never silently patched.
5 · Key technical workflows
5.1 Pick generation
For each slate the analyst fans out parallel research sub-agents (one per sport/tournament group), each
gathering matchups, current odds, model/expert probabilities, form, injuries and fatigue. Results are
synthesised into a probability estimate per candidate, compared against the no-vig implied probability to
compute edge/EV, and filtered through confidence and data-sufficiency gates. Outputs carry an advised
price, a minimum acceptable price (below which the human skips → the bet voids rather than counts),
stake in units, evidence for and against, and a confidence grade.
5.2 Settlement (the hardest engineering problem here)
Confirming a final score reliably is genuinely non-trivial: sports aggregators serve stale caches, "decoy"
recaps of prior meetings rank highly in search, and some feeds show false placeholders. The desk
enforces a two-source, dateline-verified rule — a result is only booked when two independent sources
agree on it and the article dateline / opponent match the actual fixture. A large "source-reliability flag"
archive encodes which providers can be trusted for which task and which are cache offenders. Target: no
finished bet unsettled beyond 45 minutes during a live window. Settlement writes to ledger + CSV +
dashboard atomically in one run.
5.3 Self-grading: CLV and calibration
The desk captures a near-close price for every bet to compute closing-line value (did the market move
toward or against the pick?), and tracks a Brier score of its probability estimates against the market's
implied probabilities. These are the real performance measures — win rate alone is explicitly rejected,
because a lucky win on bad reasoning is logged as a poor decision and a well-reasoned loss is not.
5.4 Supervision
Watchdog agents run management-by-exception: they check pending-bet freshness, deadline trackers,
agent heartbeats and dashboard age, escalate by severity (minor → operational → material → critical),

6 · Governance & safety model
Never-edit records. Losses and voids are permanent; history is append-only with audit entries. One
hidden loss would destroy the product's entire premise.
No end-to-end control. Selection, publication, settlement and reporting are owned by different agents
by design.
No auto-placement. Agents never place bets or move funds; a human executes and reports actual
fills. This keeps the only irreversible action under human control.
Responsible gambling is built in, not bolted on: unit staking with hard caps, no loss-chasing / no
stake-scaling to recover, a banned-claims list (no "guaranteed", "risk-free", "cannot lose"), and a
distress protocol that signposts support instead of picks. Monetisation is gated behind a hard bar (90+
days of unbroken record AND n ≥ 200 settled bets AND positive CLV AND non-negative P/L) — until
then the full product is free.

8 · Engineering challenges worth knowing about
Source trust & cache defeat. Much of the settlement logic is really an information-verification problem
— beating stale caches and decoy content with dateline + opponent + two-source checks.
Idempotent, atomic multi-file writes. Ledger, CSV and dashboard must stay consistent even though
each agent run is stateless and may overlap another.
Race conditions between overlapping runs. "Last writer reconciles" plus changed-only logging is the
current mitigation; the weekly audit is the backstop.
Scheduling cost vs coverage. Three hourly jobs alone are ~72 agent runs/day. That density is what
drives reliability — but it also consumes the account's usage budget, and when that budget is
exhausted the scheduled runs fail silently. Trimming overnight hours roughly halves the load with little
coverage loss.
Known live constraint: because every firing is a full agent session, a busy schedule can hit the account's
usage/credit ceiling, after which scheduled runs stop producing until the limit resets or the cadence is trimmed.
This is the main operational limiter of the current design, and the clearest lever for a developer to tune.
